# Continuity as Mathematical Idealization, Discreteness as Physical Instantiation? A Critical Assessment of Continuous Spacetime


Zou, Zhi Kai 邹志凯

Email:zhiyan.zou@foxmail.com Wuhan; China

https://orcid.org/0009-0000-4279-1064 


### Abstract: 

It is widely assumed that continuous mathematics is not merely a computational tool but a faithful description of physical reality. This paper argues the opposite: no known physical object satisfies the mathematical definition of continuity, and the divergences associated with continuous models—ultraviolet divergences, curvature singularities, and infinite interaction paradoxes—suggest that continuity lacks physical instantiation. Discrete models, by contrast, align better with observation and are naturally programmable. The strongest objection—that discrete spacetime cannot realize Lorentz symmetry—is addressed by showing that random topological disorder at the Planck scale can statistically average out anisotropy, just as disordered crystals approximate isotropy at scales far smaller than the atomic-to-observable gap. The paper concludes that the continuity–discreteness question remains open, but that continuity should be treated as an effective idealization rather than an ontological commitment.

Keywords: discrete spacetime, continuity, Lorentz symmetry, numerical relativity, ultraviolet divergence, mathematical ontologism, statistical isotropy, Planck-scale discreteness

### I. The Absence of Physical Instantiation of Mathematical Continuity

In the real world, no physical object has been found that satisfies the mathematical definition of continuity—that is, no known instance in reality exhibits the kind of infinite approximability that continuity entails. The mathematical continuum, as formalized by the completeness of the real numbers, requires that between any two points there exist infinitely many intermediate points, and that every approximating sequence converges to a limit within the space. No physical measurement, however precise, has ever resolved such infinite structure. Every observed object, every detected field, every measured interval appears to be finite, bounded, and ultimately discrete at some scale.
This absence is not merely an experimental limitation. It is a structural mismatch between the mathematical definition of continuity and the ontology of physical objects. Continuity is a property of formal systems, not of measured systems. The fact that no physical instantiation has been found does not by itself prove that reality is discrete, but it does undermine the assumption that continuity is a necessary feature of the physical world.

### II. Why Continuous Mathematics Works Despite Being an Idealization

The continuous mathematical framework based on the completeness of real numbers has been highly successful and effective in engineering and physical computations most of the time. It provides a powerful tool for human engineering design and calculation. This success, however, does not entail that reality is continuous. It shows only that even for discrete objects, as long as certain conditions are met, continuous mathematics can be used for computation in an efficient, effective, and practical way.
The effectiveness of continuous mathematics is therefore best understood as a computational convenience rather than an ontological revelation. It is an approximation scheme that works when the discreteness scale is far below the resolution of interest. In such regimes, the discrete substrate can be treated as a continuum without introducing measurable error. The success of the approximation is a statement about the approximation, not about the substrate.

### III. Computational Evidence from Numerical Relativity

In the computer age, because computers are binary at their core and programming languages rely on discrete objects, one simple example is numerical relativity—based on differential geometry—whose computational kernels are actually discretized approximations. Yet the results from numerical relativity already closely match those from continuous differential geometry. This in turn suggests that there is no insurmountable barrier between discrete and continuous mathematics when it comes to computation.
Numerical relativity is a particularly instructive case because it is built on the language of continuous differential geometry—manifolds, metrics, curvature tensors—yet its actual implementation is discrete. The Einstein field equations are solved on a finite grid, with finite difference or finite element approximations replacing derivatives. The results converge to the continuous solution as the grid is refined, but at no point is the computation itself continuous. The fact that discrete computation can reproduce continuous predictions to high accuracy demonstrates that discreteness and continuity are not mutually exclusive at the level of observable consequences.

### IV. The Ontological Misstep: Mathematical Ontologism

In the author's view, the philosophy of discrete mathematics is more about trying to make mathematics a faithful mapping of reality. There is also the practical advantage of programmability. Continuous mathematics, on the other hand, is based on the convenience of manual computation using calculus and similar tools. It is internally consistent and rigorous within its own axiomatic system, but it has no instantiation in physics.
There was a subtle shift in the last century: some in the mathematical and physical communities argued that since continuous mathematics is so effective, our world is likely indeed continuous. This philosophy implicitly treats continuous mathematics as reality itself—a kind of mathematical ontologism—and is not grounded in the primary principle of aligning with phenomena. In short, these scholars believe that even though no continuous object has been found in reality, reality must still be continuous—which assumes that their continuous mathematical model is complete. This view overlooks the fact that human rationality is inherently limited, and it is therefore difficult to justify as a foundational principle.

### V. Internal Inconsistencies of Continuous Models

The continuous representation of reality also brings obvious internal inconsistencies. The assumption of infinite approximability leads to divergences in physical formulas—ultraviolet divergences, curvature divergences at singularities, and paradoxes of infinite interactions. These paradoxes directly violate global energy conservation and contradict the first law of thermodynamics, which is a foundational principle of physics. Physicists have therefore had to introduce various measures to patch this bug. Continuous models also give rise to other problems, such as non-locality in heat diffusion equations.
These inconsistencies are not merely technical inconveniences. They are symptoms of a deeper mismatch between the continuous framework and the physical world. Ultraviolet divergences arise because the continuum allows arbitrarily small wavelengths, which in turn allow arbitrarily high energies. Curvature singularities arise because the continuum allows arbitrarily large curvature at a point. Infinite interaction paradoxes arise because the continuum allows infinitely many degrees of freedom in a finite region. Each of these problems is resolved in practice by introducing a cutoff, a regularization, or a renormalization procedure—that is, by discretizing the continuum in disguise.
This already hints that reality is likely not continuous, yet due to the computational convenience and general accuracy of continuous mathematics in most cases, a considerable proportion of the physics community still believes the world is continuous.

### VI. The Strongest Objection: Lorentz Symmetry on a Discrete Network

The strongest current defense of "reality is continuous" comes from its proponents, who emphasize that if reality were discrete, Lorentz symmetry could not be realized on a discrete network. This objection is serious but not decisive.
For example, randomly disordered crystalline materials can already achieve a very close approximation to isotropy, and the scale gap between atoms and observable dimensions in such materials is far smaller than the gap between the Planck scale and observable scales. No matter how far one looks, random disorder will statistically average out the "tiny asymmetries" in topological adjacency at the Planck scale, making anisotropy undetectable in observation.
The analogy is instructive. In a disordered crystal, the atomic lattice has preferred directions at the atomic scale, yet at macroscopic scales the material appears isotropic because the disorder averages out the directional preferences. The same logic applies to a discrete spacetime network: if the topological adjacency at the Planck scale is randomly disordered, then any directional preference will be statistically averaged out at scales far above the Planck scale. The anisotropy is not absent; it is hidden by statistical averaging.
This does not prove that Lorentz symmetry is exact at all scales. It shows only that a discrete network need not produce observable Lorentz violation, provided the disorder is sufficiently random and the scale gap is sufficiently large. The objection therefore does not establish continuity; it establishes only that a discrete model must satisfy certain statistical conditions to be phenomenologically viable.

### VII. The Practical Limits of Testability

If spacetime is discrete and randomly disordered at the Planck scale, high-energy particles propagating over cosmological distances could in principle accumulate small direction-dependent effects. However, the orders-of-magnitude gap between the Planck scale and the human observational scale is so enormous that random disorder would statistically average out the topological adjacency asymmetries at the Planck scale, suppressing any residual anisotropy far below the sensitivity of current experiments. This picture therefore can be neither directly confirmed nor directly falsified by existing experiments. Current high-energy cosmic-ray and gamma-ray observations can only place upper bounds on certain forms of Lorentz violation, thereby excluding a portion of the parameter space, but they cannot confirm or refute discrete spacetime within the remaining parameter space. The debate between discreteness and continuity will thus remain at the philosophical level for the foreseeable future. The author therefore argues that both the physics community and the philosophy community should keep this question open, rather than mistaking the effectiveness of continuous mathematics for proof that reality is continuous.

### VIII. Conclusion: An Open Question, Not an Ontological Commitment

Whether the world is continuous or discrete remains an open question. Neither side can yet reach a definitive conclusion. This issue will only be better understood when nature itself provides more clues.
What can be concluded is that continuity should be treated as an effective idealization rather than an ontological commitment. The success of continuous mathematics is a statement about the utility of the approximation, not about the structure of reality. The divergences and inconsistencies of continuous models suggest that continuity is not fundamental. The computational success of discrete methods suggests that discreteness is at least as viable a framework. And the statistical isotropy of disordered systems suggests that the strongest objection to discrete spacetime—Lorentz violation—can be addressed without abandoning discreteness.
The question is not whether continuous mathematics is useful. It is whether usefulness entails truth. The answer, on the present evidence, is no.
